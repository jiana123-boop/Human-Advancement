SECTION 01

Science, Technology & Nation-Building

The Philippines: pre-colonial, colonial, and post-colonial periods

01

Pre-Colonial Period

Slide 3 · PDF p. 1
  • Herbal medicines were used to treat illnesses.
  • Writing, numerical, measurement, and calendar systems were used for trading.
  • Farming, fishing, mining, and weaving were among the first livelihood skills developed.
  • Banawe Rice Terraces are mentioned as an example.

Spanish Colonial Period

Slide 4 · PDF p. 1
  • Formal education institutions were developed and scientific organizations were launched.
  • Medicine and biology were taught in educational and training institutions.
  • Natives were trained to use innovative approaches in farming.
  • Engineering was introduced and developed.

American Colonial Period

Slide 5 · PDF p. 2
  • Institutions for science and technology were recognized.
  • Laboratorio Municipal was replaced by the Bureau of Government Laboratories, then changed to the Bureau of Science.
  • The National Research Council of the Philippines was established in 1933.
  • Development focused on agriculture, medicine and pharmacy, food processing, and forestry.
  • In 1946, the Bureau of Science was replaced by the Institute of Science.

SECTION 05

Technology & the Future of Humanity

What technology is and how humans relate to it

05

Technology as an Augmentation

Slide 20 · PDF p. 5
  • The module quotes Mark Zuckerberg describing a technological tool as something that takes a human sense or ability and makes it more powerful.
  • His example is contact lenses or glasses that enhance vision.

The Essence of Technology

Slide 21 · PDF p. 6
  • Science and technology continuously modernize society.
  • Their omnipresence must not overlap the basic tenets of ethics and morality.
  • Martin Heidegger (1977), The Question Concerning Technology, defined technology as instrumental and anthropological.

Instrumental: A Means to an End

Slide 22 · PDF p. 6
  • Technology is a means to an end, not an end in itself.
  • Concerned with how things get done.
  • Technology is a tool individuals, groups, and communities can use to make an impact on society.

Anthropological: A Human Activity

Slides 23–24 · PDF p. 6
  • Technology is understood as a human activity.
  • Human activity includes achieving an end and producing or using the means to that end.
  • Technological equipment, tools, machines, inventions, their purpose, and their functions are part of this understanding.
  • The module notes that neither “technology as tool” nor “technology as human activity” reaches the true essence of technology.

Technology as a Way of Revealing

Slide 25 · PDF p. 7
  • Heidegger: the true can be pursued by the correct; what is correct leads to what is true.
  • He envisioned technology as a way of revealing.
  • Poiesis — “bringing forth”.

Modern Technology & Nature

Slide 26 · PDF p. 7
  • Modern technology challenges nature to reveal itself.
  • Nature is treated as a standing-reserve that can be disposed of by those who bind it.
  • The slide describes humans as modern technology’s tool.

Enframing & Destining

Slide 27 · PDF p. 7
  • Enframing — modern technology challenges humans to enframe nature.
  • Destining — humans are exploited as an instrument of technology for the enframing of nature.
  • Calculative thinking — a desire to put order to nature.
  • Meditative thinking — allows nature to reveal itself.

The Dangers of Technology

Slide 28 · PDF p. 7
  • Danger lies in how humans allow themselves to be consumed by technology.
  • The module says nature would remain even if humans ceased to exist.
  • Humans must not be consumed by technology or lose the essence of who they are.

The Sword of Damocles

Slide 29 · PDF p. 8
  • The Sword of Damocles is presented as an analogy for technology in a dangerous world.

SECTION 07

Biotechnology

Traditional and modern applications and aims

07

Biotechnology: Definition

Slide 36 · PDF p. 9
  • The use of living organisms in any form for the convenience of life.

Traditional Biotechnology

Slide 37 · PDF p. 10
  • Used in food preservation and the production of foods.
  • Drying / dehydrating removes enough moisture so bacteria, yeast, and molds cannot grow.
  • Examples: dried fish and dried fruits.
  • Fermentation uses microorganisms to produce a product.
  • Examples: wine, kimchi, atsara/atchara, and pickles.

Modern Biotechnology

Slide 38 · PDF p. 10
  • Provides scientists with molecular tools for better understanding the structure and function of genes in living organisms.

Aims of Modern Biotechnology

Slides 39–40 · PDF p. 10
  • Develop new precision tools and diagnostics.
  • Speed up breeding gains and efficiency.
  • Develop pest- and disease-resistant crops.
  • Combat salinity, drought, and other problems in agriculture.
  • Enhance food’s nutritional quality.
  • Increase crop varieties and choices.
  • Reduce inputs and production costs.
  • Increase profits (Bragdon et al., 2005, as cited in the module).
SECTION 08

Genetically Modified Organisms

Definitions, stated benefits, concerns, and examples

08

GMOs / Transgenic Organisms

Slide 41 · PDF p. 11
  • GMOs are products of artificial manipulation and alteration of a species’ genetic material in a laboratory using genetic engineering.
  • They are modified using recombinant DNA (gene splicing), gene modification, or transgenic technology.
  • Genes from plants, animals, bacteria, and viruses may be combined or crossbred to produce combinations that do not naturally occur in the environment.
  • Also called transgenic organisms because the process involves transfer of genes.
  • GMOs aim to address food security, agriculture, drug production, and nutrition.
  • Herbert Boyer and Stanley Cohen are identified as the first to genetically modify an organism by combining genes from two different E. coli.

GMO Pros & Cons

Slide 42 · PDF p. 11
  • Pros: increased crop yield and pest resistance.
  • Concerns: effects on consumers’ health and the environment.
  • Concerns about human rights.
  • The module warns that accidentally genetically engineering a virus or bacterium could cause a serious epidemic.

Humulin & bST

Slide 43 · PDF p. 11
  • Humulin — described as the first GMO approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), an insulin produced with genetically engineered E. coli.
  • Bovine somatotropin (bST) — FDA-approved metabolic protein hormone used to increase milk production in dairy cows for commercial use.

Flavr Savr & Crops

Slide 43 · PDF p. 11
  • Flavr Savr — FDA-approved tomato for grocery sale with delayed ripening and longer shelf life than natural tomatoes.
  • Other examples listed: Bt potatoes, Bt corn, and Roundup Ready soybeans.

Golden Rice

Slide 44 · PDF p. 11
  • Developed in the Philippines to address vitamin A deficiency, a public-health issue where rice is a staple food.
  • A variety of Oryza sativa genetically modified to make beta-carotene, a precursor of vitamin A, in the edible part of the rice.

Yorkshire Pig & Bt-Toxin Reference

Slide 44 · PDF p. 11
  • Yorkshire pig — genetically modified to produce offspring whose saliva contains phytase, an enzyme that digests plant phosphorus.
  • The slide also mentions Bt toxins in the blood of pregnant women in eastern Quebec, without further explanation.
SECTION 09

Cloning, Gene Editing & Reproduction

Biotechnology processes and applications from the module

09

Cloning

Slide 45 · PDF p. 12
  • Cloning makes identical copies of an organism.
  • A copy, or clone, has the same genetic material as the original organism.

Genetic Engineering

Slides 45–46 · PDF p. 12
  • In contrast to cloning, genetic engineering manipulates genes to create purposefully different versions of organisms, sometimes entirely new living things.
  • Genes can be introduced from one species into another.
  • The module includes a diagram of biotechnology via gene cloning.

Medical Technologies: Targeted Medications

Slide 47 · PDF p. 12
  • Identified in the module as a medical-technology category: targeted medications.

C.R.I.S.P.R. / CRISPR-Cas9

Slide 48 · PDF p. 12
  • CRISPR stands for Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats.
  • The module uses CRISPR-Cas9 as its shorthand.
  • CRISPR refers to DNA snippets of viruses in bacterial DNA; the new DNA becomes part of the bacterial DNA.
  • Cas9 is an enzyme that works like molecular scissors, cutting strands of DNA.
  • A gene-editing tool uses a CRISPR DNA sequence with the protein Cas9 to edit gene base pairs.
  • It was adapted from natural defenses of bacteria and archaea that destroy DNA from viruses and other foreign bodies.
  • The module presents applications in repairing genetic defects and using genetically modified human cells as therapies.

Seven Diseases Listed for CRISPR

Slide 49 · PDF p. 13
  • The slide lists seven diseases under the heading “7 Diseases that can be cured by CRISPR”: cancer, blood disorders, and blindness.
  • Also listed: AIDS, cystic fibrosis, muscular dystrophy, and Huntington’s disease.

In Vitro Fertilization & the Three-Parent Baby

Slides 51–52 · PDF p. 13
  • The slide includes a diagram of the in vitro fertilization process.
  • A three-parent baby is described as human offspring produced using genetic material from one man and two women.
  • The module refers to assisted reproductive technologies, especially mitochondrial manipulation or replacement and three-person in vitro fertilization (IVF).
SECTION 10

Synthetic Biology & Health Technologies

Medical testing, biosensors, and e-medicine

10

Synthetic Biology

Slide 53 · PDF p. 14
  • Design and construction of new biological parts, devices, and systems.
  • Redesign of existing natural biological systems for useful purposes.
  • Described as an engineer’s approach to biology.

Coronavirus Diagnostic Testing

Slide 54 · PDF p. 14
  • The module, citing the US FDA, distinguishes diagnostic tests and antibody tests.
  • A diagnostic test indicates whether a person has an active coronavirus infection.
  • Molecular tests (e.g. RT-PCR) detect the virus’s genetic material.
  • Antigen tests detect specific proteins on the surface of the virus.

Coronavirus Antibody Testing

Slide 54 · PDF p. 14
  • Antibody tests look for antibodies made by the immune system in response to a threat, such as a virus.
  • Antibodies can take days or weeks to develop and can remain for weeks or longer after recovery.
  • The slide cautions that antibody tests should not diagnose an active coronavirus infection.

Personal Health Monitoring (PHM)

Slide 56 · PDF p. 14
  • Wearable biosensors are portable devices worn to record and monitor health-related data outside the hospital.
  • Applications: remote patient monitoring, training support for athletes, and monitoring individuals working with hazardous elements.
  • Used to track professional drivers’ vital signs to alert them to fatigue and drowsiness.

Modern-Day Telecommunication

Slide 57 · PDF p. 15
  • The slide identifies e-medicine and online consultation as modern-day telecommunication applications.
SECTION 11

Nanotechnology

Meaning and consumer applications

11

Nanotechnology: Definition

Slide 58 · PDF p. 15
  • Control of matter on the atomic and molecular scale.
  • The module describes things about 100 nanometers across.
  • Also refers to manipulation of matter on an atomic to subatomic scale.

Everyday Consumer Products

Slides 59–60 · PDF p. 15
  • Nanotechnology appears in products such as clothing and skin lotion.
  • Silver nanoparticles in fabric kill bacteria, making clothing odor-resistant.
  • Nanoparticles in skincare deliver vitamins deeper into the skin.
  • Lithium-ion batteries can use nanoparticle-based electrodes to power plug-in electric cars.
  • Coating furniture foam with carbon nanofibers produces a flame retardant.
SECTION 12

Social Implications & Ethical Considerations

Benefits, risks, and issues raised by the module

12

Designer Babies

Slide 62 · PDF p. 16
  • A designer baby is described as a baby genetically engineered in vitro for selected traits, or whose genetic makeup was altered or selected for a desired genome.
  • Pre-implantation genetic diagnosis — selecting the best egg, described in the slide as the simplest method and used since 1988.
  • The module raises the ethical question of this technology.

Potential Uses of Cloning

Slide 63 · PDF p. 16
  • May help in-depth research, such as studies of motor neuron disease.
  • Cloned embryonic stem cells may produce tissues or organs to replace or repair damaged ones.
  • The module suggests cloning may offer parents who lost a child a way to address their loss using the deceased child’s DNA.

Religious & Ethical Issues in Cloning

Slide 64 · PDF p. 16
  • Religious concern described as “playing God”.
  • The view that a human embryo should be left alone.
  • Concern that cloning takes away the uniqueness of an individual.

Medical Issues in Cloning

Slide 65 · PDF p. 17
  • The module reports a 0.1%–3% success rate.
  • An enucleated egg and transferred nucleus may be incompatible.
  • Pregnancy may fail.
  • Problems may arise during later development.
  • Abnormal gene expressions.

Cloning Rules Listed by Country

Slide 66 · PDF p. 17
  • Australia: prohibited human cloning; therapeutic cloning is legal.
  • Canada: prohibits human cloning, stem cells, growing human embryos for research, and buying or selling human reproductive materials, according to the slide.
  • India: no specific law, but guidelines prohibit human cloning; stem-cell research is allowed.
  • Argentina: prohibits experiments involving human-cell cloning to generate human beings.

Issues on Targeted Medications

Slide 67 · PDF p. 17
  • Administration and storage require skill.
  • May produce toxicity symptoms.
  • Drug stability in dosage form is hard to maintain.
  • Requires highly sophisticated formulation technology.
  • Insufficient localization of targeted systems into tumor cells.
  • Very costly.

Issues on eMedicine & eConsultation

Slide 68 · PDF p. 17
  • Regulatory and industry barriers.
  • Limited physical examination.
  • Expensive equipment and technology.

Issues on Wearable Biosensors

Slide 69 · PDF p. 18
  • Privacy: leakage of private information and surveillance.
  • Overreliance: may reduce interactions between the wearer and their physician.
  • Reliability and accuracy concerns.

Issues on Nanotechnology

Slide 70 · PDF p. 18
  • Public trust and potential risks.
  • Potential environmental impacts.
